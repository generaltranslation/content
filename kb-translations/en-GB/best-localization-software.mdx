If you search &#39;best localisation software&#39; right now, you&#39;ll find a dozen articles written by localisation vendors, each ranking themselves first. They compare feature matrices, list pros and cons, and conclude (surprise!) that you should choose them.

We&#39;re not going to do that. Instead, we&#39;ll explain why the entire category is solving the wrong problem, and what solving the right one looks like.

## The localisation stack is broken into three

Shipping a multilingual app in 2026 requires stitching together three completely separate systems:

1. **An i18n library** — handles string extraction, interpolation, pluralisation and locale routing inside your codebase. It renders translations. It doesn&#39;t produce them.
2. **A translation management system (TMS)** — stores translation files, manages workflows and coordinates handoffs. It sits between your code and your translators but doesn&#39;t do the translating.
3. **A language service provider (LSP)** — the human translators, agencies or machine translation engines that actually produce the translated content. They don&#39;t know anything about your code or your TMS.

The library knows nothing about your translations. The TMS knows nothing about your code. The translation provider knows nothing about either. Wiring them together takes weeks of engineering time up front, then drains hours every sprint on syncing files, resolving merge conflicts, debugging webhooks and babysitting a pipeline that was never designed to be one.

## What&#39;s wrong with traditional TMS platforms

Legacy translation management systems were built for a pre-AI world. They assume a specific workflow: content is extracted from your codebase, uploaded to a dashboard, assigned to translators, reviewed, approved, exported and re-integrated. It works some of the time, but it&#39;s slow, expensive and fragile.

**They manage files, not code.** A TMS ingests your translation files (JSON, XLIFF, PO, etc.) and gives you tools to manage them. But the real source of truth is your codebase: your components, your variables, your UI hierarchy. Managing the files without understanding the code means context is always lost in transit.

Most platforms have added AI/MT features in the last few years, usually by routing strings through third-party machine translation engines and letting you pick which one to use. The AI sees isolated strings. It doesn&#39;t see your component tree, your variable names or the surrounding UI. That&#39;s why MT output still needs heavy editing.

**They scale by adding people, not by removing work.** The TMS business model is built around coordinating human translators: roles, permissions, review chains, approval workflows. That&#39;s necessary for regulated enterprise content. For a dev team shipping a SaaS app in 20 languages, it&#39;s pure overhead.

And pricing? Several of the most popular platforms make you speak to sales before you can even see figures. Others charge per-seat fees that balloon as your team grows. Unpredictable costs make it hard to budget for new languages.

## What&#39;s wrong with standalone i18n libraries

Popular i18n libraries for React and Next.js handle rendering well: interpolation, pluralisation, locale routing, Server Component support.

**But they don&#39;t translate anything.**

They render whatever you give them. You still need to:

* Extract every translatable string into JSON files
* Maintain a separate file for every locale
* Either translate those files manually, hire translators or plug in a TMS
* Keep every file in sync as your codebase changes

At 10 languages and 500 keys, that&#39;s 5,000 entries to maintain. At 50 languages, it&#39;s 25,000. The library doesn&#39;t help you with any of that. It just renders whatever you give it – half the problem, at best.

## Why most translation AI gets it wrong

Most localisation AI translates strings one at a time, in isolation. &quot;Apple&quot; could be the fruit or the company. &quot;Cell&quot; could be biological or a spreadsheet. Without context, the AI guesses.

General Translation&#39;s AI sees the full context of your codebase: component hierarchy, variable names, surrounding UI and your product glossary. The output is idiomatic, with no manual tidying up required. For edge cases, you can [add explicit context](/docs/react/reference/components/t#props) directly in the component.
