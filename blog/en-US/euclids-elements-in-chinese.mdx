*39.90° N, 116.37° E · Beijing · 1606*

Nearly 2,000 years after Euclid's *Elements* was written, a Jesuit and a Hanlin Academy scholar sat down to translate the work into Chinese for the first time. The Chinese language did not have terms for *axiom*, *proof*, *definition*, or *theorem* as mathematical concepts. Classical Chinese mathematics had developed as a system of problems, answers, and procedures, rather than as an axiomatic-deductive system.[^1]

Matteo Ricci, an Italian Jesuit missionary, carried Euclid’s *Elements* with him for nearly twenty years. He worked his way inland from Macau in 1582, slowly being accepted into the inner circles of government and intellectual life. The Latin edition he carried was edited by his former teacher in Rome, Christopher Clavius. The text had passed through Arabic in Baghdad and back into Latin in Toledo, before Renaissance scholars corrected it against the original Greek.[^2]

Xu Guangqi 徐光啟, a Chinese scholar-official, had passed the highest imperial examination just two years before and read no Latin. They worked by discussion: Ricci read and explained in spoken Chinese, and Xu wrote in the classical language. They finished the first six books on plane geometry, but circumstances intervened. The last nine took another 250 years, and went by way of a separate English translation from London.[^3]

We think of math as universal, a series of immutable truths. But the development has been different across languages and partly created through translation.

## Στοιχεία

*31.200° N, 29.919° E · Alexandria · 300 BCE*

Euclid wrote Στοιχεία, known as *Elements*, circa 300 BCE in Ancient Greece. It was copied by hand on scrolls; Euclid's original is lost, and the oldest surviving pieces are papyrus fragments. It is undoubtedly one of the most important works of Western history. It established a system of definitions, axioms, and propositions, "which has served as a model of rigorous mathematical reasoning for more than two thousand years, and perhaps will last forever."[^4]

{/* Image: fragment */}

*Elements* is a 13-book treatise on plane and solid geometry and number theory which includes the Pythagorean theorem, Thales's theorem, the Euclidean algorithm, the proof that primes are infinite, and the five regular solids.[^5]

Very few texts have been passed to the modern world through such a complex path of editions, translations, revisions, and abridgements.[^6]

The original text is particularly hard to recover, even more so than other ancient Greek works of literature, philosophy, and history. Because Euclid's *Elements* was used as a textbook (the world’s oldest continuously used math textbook), **teachers felt free to make liberal additions, corrections, and omissions.**[^7]

The translation history is intricate. The first translation of *Elements* was made in Arabic by the mathematician al-Ḥajjāj (الحجّاج بن يوسف بن مطر) in 9th-century Baghdad (around 800 CE).[^8]

{/* Image: Latin */}

**The Arabic translation was then used for the Latin translation**, made by Adelard of Bath in 1130. Medieval Europe thus became acquainted with Euclid through Latin translations of Arabic translations of the original ancient Greek. A later Latin version in the same tradition, by Campanus of Novara, became the first printed edition of *Elements*, published in 1482 in Venice.[^9]

{/* Image: Latin */}

{/* Image: printed */}

Conveying the mathematical depth of Euclid’s work, with its koan-like sentences and dense logical intricacy, is a challenge in itself. The earliest English translation, published in 1570 by Henry Billingsley (a character who will appear later), included **hand-glued, three-dimensional paper pop-up models** to help readers visualize geometric solids in Book XI.[^10]

{/* Image: 3D pop-ups */}

And Oliver Byrne’s 1847 edition became a landmark of Victorian book design, using primary colors to visually demonstrate geometric proofs. The elegant designs look incredibly modern and even seem to prefigure the work of decades-later Bauhaus and De Stijl artists, Piet Mondrian and László Moholy-Nagy among them.[^11]

{/* Image: Byrne colors */}

## Journey to Peking

*22.194° N, 113.543° E · Macau · 1582*

Matteo Ricci was **the first Westerner in history invited into the Forbidden City**. He drew the first world map in China, brought mechanical clocks to the court, and taught astronomy and science. After his death, he was the first foreigner granted a burial site in Peking by imperial decree.[^12]

But when he arrived in 1582 in Macau, a Portuguese trading post, he was not permitted to live in the country’s interior. Each step inland depended on an official’s permission. He thus moved slowly towards the capital, Peking, adopting the Chinese language and cultural customs and the Chinese name Li Madou 利瑪竇. **It took him nearly twenty years.** He moved from Macau (Aomen 澳門) to Zhaoqing 肇慶, Shaozhou 韶州, Nanchang 南昌, Nanjing 南京, and finally Peking (Beijing 北京) in 1601.[^13]

He traveled with his precious books, including his copy of the 1574 Latin translation of Euclid’s *Elements*, which his former mathematics and astronomy teacher Christopher Clavius had edited.[^14]

{/* Image: Clavius */}

He first met Xu Guangqi in Nanjing. In 1604, Xu succeeded in the Imperial Examination (*kējǔ* 科舉) and became a member of the Hanlin Academy (Hanlin Yuan 翰林院), after which the two became close friends.[^15]

In the autumn of 1606, they began to translate Ricci’s Latin translation of Euclid’s *Elements*.[^16]

Their method is printed under the title of the text: 泰西利瑪竇口譯，吳淞徐光啟筆受 (orally translated by Li Madou of the Far West, received with the brush by Xu Guangqi of Wusong).[^17] **Ricci explained the Latin in spoken Chinese, which Xu then formulated into classical Chinese.** They finished the first six books and published them under the title *Jǐhé yuánběn* 幾何原本 (*Elements of Geometry*) a year later. They decided to study Chinese reception before translating the other books. But Ricci died in 1610, before they could finish.[^18]

{/* Image: Chinese */}

The remaining seven books were not translated into Chinese **until 250 years later.** In 1857, the British missionary Alexander Wylie (Weilie Yali 偉烈亞力) and the Chinese mathematician Li Shanlan 李善蘭 finally completed the translations in Shanghai. It was again done with oral translation and discussion, this time from the English translation by Henry Billingsley, which was made from the original Greek.[^19]

(Billingsley himself has a fascinating history as a Cambridge-educated merchant, chief customs officer, and later Lord Mayor of London.[^20] Scholars originally doubted he could have made the landmark translation himself. But his own copy of the Greek text, expertly annotated in his handwriting, survived and was identified in 1879 in the Princeton University Library.[^21])

{/* Image: Billingsley */}

The translation of Euclid's *Elements* was finally completed in Chinese **more than two thousand years after it was originally written.**[^22]

## The Chinese mathematical system

*39.90° N, 116.37° E · Beijing · 1606*

Of course, the Chinese had their own mathematics. Around the same time as Euclid, the Chinese developed arithmetic and elementary geometry. In fact, even as early as the fourth century BCE, counting rods were used for calculating — **effectively implementing a decimal place-value system.** This counting rod method is not known to have been used by any other ancient civilization.[^23]

But ancient Chinese mathematics developed quite differently than Greek mathematics. Chinese mathematics was **motivated by problems of calendar, trade, land, agriculture, government, and architecture.** It was more focused on algebra and practical applications rather than geometry and theoretical reasoning. Like the language, it was very concise.[^24]

The core text of classical Chinese mathematics, *Jiǔzhāng suànshù* 九章算術 (*The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*), was developed in the first century CE. While Euclid moves from definition to theorem, **the *Nine Chapters* move from question to procedure**. The text contains 246 problems structured as *wèn* 問 (problem), *dá* 答 (answer), and *shù* 術 (general procedure). For example, the right-triangle relation (Pythagorean theorem) is there as the *gōugǔ* 句股 (short leg, long leg) rule:[^25]

*Wen* 問 (problem): 今有句三尺，股四尺，問為弦幾何？\
A right triangle has a short leg of 3 feet and a long leg of 4 feet. How long is the hypotenuse?

*Da* 答 (answer): 答曰：五尺。\
Answer: 5 feet.

*Shu* 術 (procedure): 句股術曰：句股各自乘，并，而開方除之，即弦。\
The leg-and-leg procedure: square each leg, add the squares, take the square root. That is the hypotenuse.[^26]

The procedure works for any right triangle. But it is given to the reader as an instruction, not as a statement or claim about all right triangles. A later commentary supplied the reasoning by slicing the two smaller squares and reassembling them into a larger square.[^27]

Euclid’s version, Proposition I.47, runs very differently. A general statement comes first (in every right triangle the square on the hypotenuse equals the two squares on the legs), then a proof built on the propositions before it. No side is given a specific length or computed value; every magnitude is only a line labeled by a letter.[^28]

{/* Image: comparing the two */}

It’s mind-bending to consider that **the deductive method is not self-evident**, as we tend to treat it centuries later, but a system of conventions. It starts by asking the reader to agree to a set of terms and unproved assumptions. In the edition Ricci carried, that meant accepting 36 definitions, 4 postulates, and 19 common notions before the first proof. In Euclid’s own text, 23, 5, and 5.[^29]

Ricci and Xu thus had to translate Euclid not only as a text but as **a genre of radically new mathematical reasoning**.[^30] Even as a text alone, translating Latin to classical Chinese poses a challenge. They **sit far apart on the language tree**, with vast differences in grammar, morphology, style, and structure.[^31] The patterns of writing in East and West are utterly different (東西文理，又自絕殊), wrote Ricci.[^32]

The pair **coined a few dozen new technical terms**: like *jièshuō* 界說 (definition), *qiúzuò* 求作 (postulate), and *gōnglùn* 公論 (axiom), although these were later replaced by terms developed in Meiji Japan and then carried back into Chinese. Their names for figures, like *diǎn* 點 (point), *zhíjiǎo* 直角 (right angle), *píngxíngxiàn* 平行線 (parallel lines), and others, survived. These terms are still found in primary-school textbooks today.[^33]

{/* Image: Chinese */}

For *jieshuo*, definition, Ricci and Xu translated the etymology, not just the word. Euclid’s term is ὅρος, *horos*, a boundary or limit. The Latin *definitio* carries the same image, from *finis* for border. *Jie* 界 means boundary, and *shuo* 說 means explanation.[^34]

The title word, *jihe* 幾何, **was actually the Chinese term for “how much.”** It was used because geometry and arithmetic were both studies of quantity. But only Ricci and Xu’s translation of the first six books, all plane geometry, circulated in China for the next 250 years, before the remaining seven books were translated.[^35]

Today, the word *jihe* 幾何 (simplified: 几何) means geometry.
