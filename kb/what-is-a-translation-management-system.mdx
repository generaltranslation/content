A TMS sits between the people who write source content and the people (and engines) who translate it. Content goes in as files or strings, gets translated and reviewed inside the TMS, and comes back out in each target language. It doesn't render anything in your app; that's the job of an i18n library.

## What a TMS does

Most TMS platforms combine the same building blocks:

- **A content repository.** Source files or individual strings, each with its translations per locale, versions, and status (untranslated, translated, reviewed).
- **Translation memory (TM).** A database of earlier source and target pairs. [Microsoft's glossary](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/globalization/reference/glossary) describes how a TM recycles existing translations by exact match or a "fuzzy" (similarity) match, which keeps repeated sentences consistent and cuts cost. TMX is the standard format for moving a TM between tools.
- **A termbase or glossary.** Approved translations of product names, feature names, and technical terms, plus terms that must never be translated. Termbases have their own exchange format, TBX. (See Microsoft's [localization guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/globalization/localization/localization).)
- **Workflow and review.** Steps such as translation, editing, and proofreading, assigned to people by language, with an editor where translators see the source, TM matches, and glossary terms side by side.
- **Machine translation, AI, and vendor connectors.** Pre-translation through machine translation (MT) engines or LLMs, and connections to language service providers who supply human translators.
- **QA checks.** Automated checks for missing placeholders, length limits, glossary violations, and inconsistent translations.
- **Integrations.** A CLI, an API, and connectors for Git hosts, CMSs, and design tools that move content in and out.

## How a TMS works in a software project

1. Engineers extract user-facing strings into resource files (JSON, YAML, `.strings`, `strings.xml`, PO) under keys, with an i18n library reading those files at run time.
2. A CLI or Git integration pushes the source files to the TMS. With Crowdin's CLI, for example, that's `crowdin upload`, configured in `crowdin.yml`.
3. The TMS pre-translates new strings from the TM and an MT engine or LLM, then routes them to translators and reviewers.
4. QA checks run, and reviewers approve.
5. The translated files come back through `crowdin download`, a sync, or a pull request, and the app ships them in its next release.

Steps 1, 2, and 5 are the parts engineers own, and the loop repeats whenever copy changes.

## Translation management system examples

Each platform describes itself a little differently:

- **[Crowdin](https://crowdin.com/)** calls itself an "AI-powered localization platform," with translation memory, glossaries, built-in QA checks, MT engines, and vendor integrations. (See [General Translation vs. Crowdin](/kb/general-translation-vs-crowdin).)
- **[Lokalise](https://lokalise.com/)** describes a localization workflow "for your web and mobile apps, games and digital content," with automated workflows that apply TM, run QA checks, and route approvals. (See [General Translation vs. Lokalise](/kb/general-translation-vs-lokalise).)
- **[Phrase](https://phrase.com/)** calls itself a "Language Intelligence Platform." Its products include Phrase TMS, a translation management system, and Phrase Strings for software localization. (See [General Translation vs. Phrase](/kb/general-translation-vs-phrase).)
- **[Smartling](https://www.smartling.com/)** calls its TMS an "all-in-one translation orchestration platform" and sells human translation from its own network of 4,000+ linguists. (See [General Translation vs. Smartling](/kb/general-translation-vs-smartling).)
- **[Transifex](https://www.transifex.com/)** describes itself as "the one localization hub for your entire process," with a per-project [translation memory](https://help.transifex.com/en/articles/6224636-introduction-to-translation-memory), glossaries, and [Transifex Native](https://developers.transifex.com/docs/native) for pushing strings from code. (See [General Translation vs. Transifex](/kb/general-translation-vs-transifex).)

Open-source and developer-oriented options such as [Weblate](/kb/general-translation-vs-weblate), [Tolgee](/kb/general-translation-vs-tolgee), and [Locize](/kb/general-translation-vs-locize) cover parts of the same ground.

## TMS vs. i18n library vs. language service provider

The three get confused because a localization program usually needs all of their jobs done:

| | i18n library | TMS | Language service provider (LSP) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Code in your app | A software platform | A company of translators and project managers |
| Examples | i18next, react-intl, next-intl | Crowdin, Lokalise, Phrase, Smartling, Transifex | Translation agencies and freelancers |
| Its job | Pick the locale, load translations, format dates, numbers, and plurals | Store strings and translations, run translation and review workflows | Translate and review content |
| Produces translations? | No | Through MT, AI, or the people it routes work to | Yes, by people |
| Who uses it | Developers | Localization managers, translators, reviewers | Your localization manager, as a vendor |

An LSP often works inside your TMS, or brings its own. Microsoft's localization guide lists the choices: an LSP, single-language or multi-language vendors, or freelancers.

## When you need a TMS, and when you don't

You probably need a TMS when:

- most of your words are translated by people, such as an agency or freelance linguists, who reuse past work from a TM inside a CAT (computer-assisted translation) editor;
- vendors send and receive work as XLIFF or Office documents;
- a localization manager runs the program across many content types and vendors.

You probably don't when:

- one or two languages and a small amount of copy can live in the repository and be translated by a teammate (see [Should you build your own i18n or use General Translation?](/kb/general-translation-vs-in-house-i18n));
- most of your text is in code, copy changes with every release, and the extract, send, wait, and merge loop is what slows you down.

## Where General Translation fits

General Translation isn't shaped like a TMS. Instead of exporting strings to a separate platform, it translates in the development workflow: General Translation's [`<T>`](/docs/react/reference/components/t) component wraps UI where it's written, so there's no resource file to extract, and the CLI's [`npx gt translate`](/docs/cli/reference/commands/translate) translates the files in your repository (JSON, YAML, Markdown and MDX, PO, iOS `.xcstrings`, Android `strings.xml`, and more) as a build step. [Locadex](/docs/platform/locadex/quickstart), General Translation's AI agent, can run the same work through pull requests on the triggers you configure.

The TMS jobs still have a home:

- **Terminology and style.** [Context Groups](/docs/platform/dashboard/guides/defining-context-for-translations) hold a Glossary for key terms and Custom Prompts for tone, formality, and conventions, shared across every project they're assigned to.
- **Context.** Each translation carries context from the code: the surrounding component, variable names, and any [`context`](/docs/react/reference/components/t#context) notes you add, so "Save" can be translated as writing to disk or as spending less money.
- **Review.** Your team reviews and edits translations in the Dashboard's [Translation Editor](/docs/platform/dashboard/guides/reviewing-translations), with no seat charges, so adding reviewers doesn't add to the bill (inviting members requires a Starter or Enterprise plan).
- **Repeat runs.** Matching translations can be reused from earlier versions of a file, and new or changed source content is translated and charged, so a run handles what changed.

General Translation doesn't support XLIFF, CSV, ARB, or DOCX files, and it doesn't sell professional human translation. If those are central to your program, a TMS is the better fit. For a side-by-side of all five platforms above, see [Why use General Translation instead of a TMS?](/kb/general-translation-vs-tms)

## Key takeaways

- A TMS stores content and translations and coordinates translating them, with translation memory, a termbase, review workflows, MT and vendor connectors, QA checks, and integrations.
- Crowdin, Lokalise, Phrase, Smartling, and Transifex are the commonly compared platforms; each pairs a TMS with AI translation and access to human translators.
- An i18n library renders translations in your app, a TMS manages producing them, and an LSP supplies the translators.
- General Translation translates inside the development workflow instead, with Glossaries, Custom Prompts, and review in the Dashboard, and leaves XLIFF exchange and professional translation services to a TMS.
